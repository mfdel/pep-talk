# Theme: `rehearsal` — walk through the event once, before it happens

Shared rules: `themes/guide.md` (read first). Length ~7 min. Default mode: wake, the morning of or
the day before. Sleep allowed the night before.

## Intent

Mental rehearsal of one specific upcoming event, step by step, seen through the listener's own eyes
and narrated as "you": arriving, the first minute, one core story landing, the known hard moment
and a calm recovery, the close. Only then a short glimpse of the outcome, paired with the actions
that produced it.

It rehearses the **approach and the recovery, not the content of answers**. For an impromptu
speaking slot that is the stand-up and the structure (one true opinion → one example → sit down),
never sample topics. Rehearsing answer content turns preparation into a permission slip: "I can
only go in once I have every answer". **One rehearsal per event**, not on repeat: replaying it
several nights before is anticipatory worry with a soundtrack.

- **Best for:** an interview, a presentation, a pitch, an exam, an audition, a performance review, a
  hard conversation, a first day, a networking coffee.
- **Not for:** a vague or distant goal (use `future-self`); an event with no known details; small
  reversible acts such as a like or a comment (just do them; a 7-minute talk before a comment is
  deliberation tax); a second rehearsal of an event already rehearsed.

## Evidence basis

| Element | Why | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Simulate the process, not just the outcome | Process simulation improved preparation and performance more than outcome simulation | Pham & Taylor 1999 |
| Mental practice of the task | Meta-analysis: mental practice has a reliable positive effect on performance, stronger for cognitive tasks | Driskell, Copper & Moran 1994 |
| Include a problem and the recovery (coping imagery) | Simulating how to handle setbacks supports coping and self-regulation | Taylor, Pham, Rivkin & Armor 1998 |
| Attention outward; one rehearsal per event | Self-focused attention and anticipatory processing maintain social fear | Clark & Wells 1995 |

## Required inputs

From brief §4 or the prompt: event type, date, place or format (video or in person, platform), who
is there (by role; names only if the profile allows), the opening line or one-line pitch, the core
story to land, the known hard moment. From brief §6 and §12: the alarm line, the recovery if-then.
From brief §1: whether this event has been rehearsed already. **If any are missing, ask before
writing. Unattended: stop and report the missing inputs.**

## Structure

Pace ≈ 130 wpm. **540–590 spoken words** (≈ 4.4 min) + **≈ 160 s of `[[PAUSE n]]`** ≈ 7 min.
Characters 3,400–4,000.

| # | Section | Words | Pause budget | What happens |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Arrive | 45–70 | ~20 s | Name, two breaths. "We walk through it once, together." |
| 2 | The morning | 50–60 | ~15 s | One routine, the prepared notes, the time of the event |
| 3 | Arriving | 70–90 | ~20 s | **Video:** the link, the camera light, the waiting screen. **In person:** the door, the reception desk, the room. The alarm rings; name it once and proceed. One `[keyboard typing]` or `[knocking on door]` cue allowed |
| 4 | The first minute + one core story | 120–150 | ~30 s | Posture, voice, their prepared opening at fair value, no hedge. Then **one core story lands**: what it is, who it is for, why it matters — from the brief, at fair value |
| 5 | The hiccup + recovery | 100–120 | ~30 s | The known hard moment from the brief. One beat of noticing the body, then **attention goes outward**: look at the person, listen to the question. The if-then plan executes. Calm return |
| 6 | The close | 50–70 | ~15 s | Their closing question or line, the goodbye, leaving the call or the room |
| 7 | Outcome glimpse + return | 60–80 | ~25 s | One or two lines of the outcome, paired with the action scoreboard. Then the mode ending |

**The hiccup** is the moment the listener dreads most, from the brief: the question that has caught
them before, the slide that always runs long, the silence after a hard sentence. Typical if-thens:

- A question that stumps you: "When a question stumps you, you say what you know, what you will
  check, and when you will come back."
- "What did you do yourself?" in an interview: "When they ask what you did, you name the thing, the
  choice you made, and what happened next." Keep the scope the profile states; never a bigger one.
- A surprise question or an impromptu slot: "one true opinion, one example, stop".
- A hard conversation: "When the other person goes quiet, you wait, and you let the silence work."

## Endings

- `wake` — count up 1 → 3, `[softly]` → plain → `[excited]`. One action for today that prepares the
  approach ("When you finish breakfast, you say the opening out loud twice"). Last pause ≤ 5 s.
- `sleep` (night before) — no count-up. Compress the hiccup to two sentences, fully resolved before
  minute 4. The outcome glimpse in `[slowly]`, then "It is prepared. You can rest." in
  `[whispering]`, last chunk `[sleepy drowsy voice]`, end on `[[PAUSE 30]]`.

## Brief must contain

- §1: has this event been rehearsed already? (one rehearsal per event).
- §4 the event file: what, date, time, video or in person, platform or place, who by role, opening
  line (from notes or marked PROPOSED), core story, the known hard moment and where it came up
  before, the recovery if-then, keep-consistent items (numbers and scope the profile states).
- §6 the alarm line in their own words, if they wrote one; §9 their own lines; §12 one or two
  if-then plans; §13 imagery to avoid; §14, §15.
- **Fallback** if the brief is `INSUFFICIENT`: ask (unattended: stop and report).

The brief agent finds this best in the invitation or calendar entry, prep notes, the CV or the
material being presented, and notes from similar events before.

## Affirmations

Two short process lines, woven in as cues rather than looped: one at the end of section 4, one at
the end of section 5. Each said twice. Their own lines first. One of them is the silence where the
listener says it themselves. Log both.

- "Ready is a feeling. Prepared is a fact. You are prepared."
- "You go in to practise, not to be perfect."
- "When the question surprises you, you give one true opinion, one example, and stop."

## Worked example — section 5, `mode: wake`, presentation to the leadership team

```
# 5. The hiccup
[slowly] Then the question comes. The finance lead asks what happens to the budget
if the launch slips by a quarter. You do not have that number in front of you.
[[PAUSE 3]]
[softly] One beat of heat in your face. The alarm is ringing.
That is the old alarm, Alex. It rings before rooms like this one. Proceeding.
[[PAUSE 3]]
[sighing] One breath. Then look at the finance lead. Listen to the question that was asked.
[[PAUSE 3]]
[slowly] When a question stumps you, you say what you know, what you will check, and when you will come back.
You answer the question asked. Then you stop talking.
[[PAUSE 4]]
[softly] You hear your own voice, level and plain. No "I think". No "maybe".
The finance lead nods and writes something down.
[[PAUSE 3]]
[slowly] The wobble came. You handled it. That is the rehearsal, and it is done.
[[PAUSE 5]]
```

## Don'ts

- No flawless run-through; there is always one hiccup and a recovery.
- No rehearsing the content of answers; the approach, the structure and the recovery only.
- No invented names, company facts, numbers or questions the brief does not support. Use "the
  interviewer", "the room", "the call", or the role the brief gives.
- No scope or numbers beyond what the profile states.
- No outcome glimpse longer than two lines; no "they offer you the job on the spot".
- Field view only: through their own eyes, not watching themselves from across the room.
- No scene cue beyond one; no `[applause]` unless the event is a talk and the mode is wake.
